Tax Residency and Cross-Border Considerations.

Living, working, investing or operating a business across more than one country can create tax questions that are more complicated than simply counting the number of days spent in each country.

Tax residency is an important concept for individuals who have connections with more than one country. It can influence how income is taxed and how domestic tax rules interact with international tax agreements.

Cyprus has specific rules under which an individual may become a Cyprus tax resident. Understanding those rules is important, but residency should be considered together with the individual's wider circumstances.

The 183-day rule

Under Cyprus tax-residency rules, an individual is generally considered a Cyprus tax resident where they spend more than 183 days in Cyprus during the relevant tax year.

The calculation of days is subject to specific rules. For example, the Cyprus Tax Department states that the day of arrival is counted as a day in Cyprus, while the day of departure is counted as a day outside Cyprus.

This makes accurate travel records particularly important for individuals whose activities involve regular international travel.

The 60-day rule

Cyprus also has a separate 60-day tax-residency route for individuals who meet the applicable conditions.

According to the Cyprus Tax Department, an individual may qualify under the 60-day rule where they remain in Cyprus for at least 60 days during the tax year and satisfy the other statutory conditions.

These conditions include relevant connections with Cyprus, such as carrying on a business and/or being employed or holding an office in Cyprus, together with maintaining a permanent residence in Cyprus.

Residency is not simply a day-count exercise

The number of days spent in Cyprus matters, but the applicable residency test also contains additional conditions that need to be considered.

What about domicile?

Tax residence and domicile are related concepts, but they are not the same thing.

The Cyprus Tax Department distinguishes tax residence for income-tax purposes from domicile for purposes of the Special Defence Contribution regime.

An individual's domicile can depend on their domicile of origin, domicile of choice and other statutory provisions. Long-term residence can also become relevant under the applicable rules.

Why cross-border situations are more complicated

A person can have connections with several countries at the same time.

Someone may live in Cyprus, own a company in another country, receive foreign investment income, maintain property abroad or spend significant periods in another jurisdiction.

In those circumstances, simply identifying Cyprus as a person's preferred residence does not necessarily answer every tax question.

The tax rules of the other country may also need to be considered, together with any applicable double-taxation agreement.

Double taxation considerations

Double taxation can arise where the same income or economic activity is potentially taxed in more than one jurisdiction.

Cyprus has a network of double-tax treaties designed to address international tax issues. The specific treatment depends on the relevant treaty, the type of income and the facts of the individual case.

This is one reason why cross-border tax questions should be reviewed based on the complete facts rather than relying on a general rule.

International business owners

Tax residency questions can also arise for entrepreneurs who establish or manage businesses across borders.

Relevant considerations may include:

  • Where the individual is tax resident.
  • Where the company is tax resident.
  • Where business activities are actually carried out.
  • Where management and decision-making take place.
  • Where income is generated.
  • Whether foreign tax has already been paid.
  • Whether a double-taxation agreement applies.

Keep evidence and records

Cross-border tax positions can depend heavily on facts and evidence.

Individuals with international activities should consider keeping appropriate records of travel, employment, business activities, residence, investments and relevant income.

Good documentation can make it easier to explain the basis of a tax position when required.

The practical takeaway

Tax residency is an important part of international tax planning, but it should not be considered in isolation.

For individuals living or working across borders, the right approach is to consider residency, domicile, income sources, business activity and applicable international rules together.

The earlier these questions are considered, the easier it can be to identify potential issues before they become compliance problems.

General information

This article is provided for general information purposes only and does not constitute legal, tax or accounting advice. Tax residency depends on the applicable legislation and the facts and circumstances of each individual. Cross-border matters may also require consideration of the laws of other jurisdictions and applicable tax treaties.
